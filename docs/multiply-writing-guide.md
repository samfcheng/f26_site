# Multiply: writing worksheet

This is an outline and interview guide for Sam, not case study copy. Answer in fragments, bullets, or rough paragraphs. Skip anything you do not remember; mark uncertainties so we can revisit them. Your wording and experiences will be the source material for the eventual page.

## Suggested story structure

Start with the mortgage workflow and your role, then develop two project chapters: the CRM and the dashboard/navigation redesign. End with a small selection of other work and specific reflections. This order is provisional: lead with whichever project has the clearest combination of personal ownership, interesting decisions, and evidence of a result.

A possible connection to explore: helping loan officers understand a borrower’s history and decide what to do next. Does that describe what you were working toward, or were these separate problems?

## First writing session

Start with these five questions. Spend around 15–20 minutes; there is no need to answer the whole worksheet at once.

1. Describe one real situation where a loan officer struggled with the old tools. What were they trying to do, what got in their way, and what happened next?
2. What did you personally own, and what did the PM, engineers, and other designer own?
3. Pick one decision you spent time wrestling with. What were the alternatives, and what finally made you choose?
4. What did someone say or do that changed your design? Describe the design before and after that moment.
5. What actually shipped, and what evidence do you have about how people used it afterward?

Your answers will help us decide where the case study needs depth.

## 01 — CRM: what changed in the officer’s workflow?

Reader takeaway: why this work mattered beyond adding more communication types.

### The starting point

- Your notes say “created a CRM,” but also mention existing notes and marketing emails. What existed already, and what exactly did you create or redesign?
- Where did loan officer and borrower emails live before? Tell one concrete story about reconstructing a conversation or finding missing context.
- How did you learn there was a problem: observation, conversations, usage data, a request, or something else?
- What did low utilization mean? Why did you think people were not using the existing tool?

### Your decisions

- What was the most consequential design decision? What alternative did you seriously consider, and why did you move away from it?
- How did you organize notes, marketing emails, loan officer emails, and borrower emails so an officer could understand the history?
- Which details needed to be immediately visible, and which could sit behind an interaction?
- What was one difficult state or edge case you actually encountered? How did you resolve it?
- What feedback or implementation constraint changed your initial approach?

### The result

- What could an officer do afterward that was difficult before? Describe the same task before and after.
- What supports “high utilization”? If measured, record the metric, population, and comparison period. If observed, describe who used it and what you saw.
- What else changed around the same time that might have affected adoption?
- What remained unresolved when your work ended?

Possible visuals: the same borrower task in the old and new designs; one discarded approach with your reason for changing it.

## 02 — Navigation and dashboard: finding tools and choosing actions

Reader takeaway: how you helped a growing product support a loan officer’s work.

### When the old structure stopped working

- What were the original one or two tools? What had been added since?
- Give a specific example of an officer having trouble finding something. What about the structure caused that difficulty?
- Were finding tools and deciding what to do next related problems? How did you identify each?

### Navigation alternatives

- Which navigation paradigms did you actually explore? For each, what worked and what broke down?
- What does “mixed nav and sidebar” mean in your design? What lived in each area, and what principle determined that split?
- Why did the chosen structure fit this product and its users?
- Was there tension between making new features prominent and keeping frequent tasks easy to reach? Describe a specific decision.

### An actionable dashboard

- What should an officer understand or do immediately after opening the dashboard?
- How did you choose which tasks and outreach suggestions to surface? Who determined their priority?
- Describe one task from appearing on the dashboard through the officer taking action. What context did they need?
- What useful-looking content did you remove or deprioritize, and why?
- Which empty, completed, or unusual state forced you to rethink the design, if any?

### Feedback and iteration

- What was Integral, who was there, and what did you present?
- What specifically revealed the need for labels in the collapsed sidebar? What tradeoff did adding them introduce?
- What was confusing or missing in recent activity? What changed, and why?
- Did you get to check whether these revisions helped? What did you observe?
- Which parts shipped, and which remained proposals or prototypes?

Possible visuals: navigation alternatives; a dashboard with a few annotations explaining priority; the sidebar or recent activity before and after feedback.
